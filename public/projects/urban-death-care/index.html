01 / Framing & context

A1, pp. 2–3, 10 ↗A2, p. 2 ↗

Why funerals?
Our starting point in urban India

We each brought 4–5 possible topics to the table: e-waste, cold storage, Asha workers, matchmaking services, and inconsistent ride-hailing were among them. We chose funeral services after comparing their potential and viability.

Our preliminary research looked at Anthyesti, Beleiv, Last Journey, Kaashimukthi, Mokshshil, and Shradhanjali. It gave us five lines of inquiry: the loss of the social safety net, material acquisition, professionalization, the burden of choice, and digital fixes.

We approached this as a “Service with novelty”, rather than a cold business segment. We wanted the system to remain invisible enough that ease, seamlessness, and dignity could stay central to the family’s experience.

Mortality data / CRS & NCRB

8.66 million

Registered deaths in India in 2023, as cited from the Civil Registration System in our A2 framing. The notes describe most deaths as natural, including non-communicable conditions such as ischemic heart disease and stroke, alongside communicable and maternal conditions.

A2 places unnatural deaths at 7–10% of mortality and cites recent NCRB figures of approximately 1.71 lakh suicides (12.3 per lakh population) and 4.3 lakh accidental deaths, led by traffic accidents, drowning, and falls. It also records a 13% rise in sudden medical-emergency deaths, including heart attacks and brain hemorrhages.

The Golden Hour

A logistical buffer

We used “Golden Hour” for the period immediately after a death, when the family’s cognitive load is highest. Our framing described the stress of 50+ uncoordinated phone calls for transport, materials, people, and paperwork.

We wanted families to become protected consumers, with support that absorbed this coordination, rather than leaving them indebted to a local broker or choosing a rigid, one-size-fits-all online package.

A subjective eye on middlemen

Understanding the existing response

We did not dismiss the unorganized sector. Although ritual brokers and middlemen are often viewed as predatory, our research found that they could provide the only speedy solution a family knew how to access, sometimes faster than an end-to-end service.

Awareness was part of the problem. Our provider persona describes organized services as less than 1% of the market; most families still navigate local, informal networks.

The boundary of our semester proposal

We focused on natural deaths, including age-related deaths and terminal illness, in Tier-1 urban Indian cities where traditional community support has weakened. We mapped the predictable transition from home or hospital to the crematorium. The outcome is an academic service proposal, developed through research and mapping within the semester; its implementation remains future work.

02 / Secondary & primary research

A1, pp. 4–9 ↗A2, pp. 3–4 ↗

Literature review & fieldwork at Hebbal Crematorium

We read 15 papers before developing our questionnaires. Four threads helped us connect ritual practices with the practical work of arranging a funeral.

Papers 1, 11 & 13

Pandemic disruption & ritual agency

COVID-19 protocols, including sealed body bags and electronic cremation, disrupted rites in Kerala and West Bengal. The literature described high-fee “COVID ritual brokers” negotiating with hospitals and crematoria.

The mixed-methods review shifted our attention from the size of a funeral to the family’s sense of control over meaningful rituals. The study on restricted mourning also described how alternative coping rituals could matter more than the changed logistics alone.

Papers 2, 6 & 14

Urban space, environment & acceptance

Our literature notes cite an estimate of 50–60 million trees consumed annually by open-air wood pyres. Architectural research proposed vertical cemeteries and multifaith centres for land-scarce cities.

Burning Issues added a historical explanation for resistance to indoor and electric crematoria: early municipal facilities were associated with unclaimed or destitute bodies, while open-air wood pyres carried ritual purity and status.

Papers 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10 & 12

Community networks & outsourced care

Accounts of baradari, the Hindu 13-day mourning period and Shraad, and Sikh Japji prayers showed how much knowledge and labour once sat within extended communities. Migration and nuclear households change that support.

One study of 150 urban respondents reported that over 80% found arranging a funeral difficult or very difficult, and nearly 60% would consider outsourcing logistics. A separate paper on funeral directors described the emotional labour of “surface acting” while serving grieving families.

Papers 9 & 15

Legal & medico-legal friction

The death-registration study tracked national completeness rising from 58% in 2000 to 81% in 2018, alongside persistent state and gender inequalities. Its discussion of lower female registration connected administrative work with patriarchal succession norms.

Research from Uttarakhand described how Jal Samadhi (water burial) and Bhu Samadhi (earth burial) can conflict with forensic and medico-legal systems when bodies surface or are unearthed.

Patterns in our survey

  1. Planning often fell to the eldest man.

    The eldest male member usually planned and budgeted the funeral.

  2. Families preferred familiar networks.

    Most respondents were unaware of, or unwilling to use, middlemen and end-to-end services; they preferred family and local community contacts.

  3. Coordination was a reason to seek help.

    Avoiding coordination stress and lacking local family support were the main reasons to consider an end-to-end service.

  4. Technology met resistance in rituals.

    Respondents were reluctant to involve technology, including digitally broadcasting a funeral to distant relatives.

From interviews to interventions

Meta Matrix & Root Cause Analysis

We used separate questionnaires for bereaved families and service providers, alongside conversations with families outside Hebbal Crematorium.

We brought the observations into a Meta Matrix and performed a Root Cause Analysis (RCA). We colour-coded interview answers against the interventions they informed, so we could trace a proposal back to what someone had told us.

03 / Personas & system mapping

A2, pp. 5–10 ↗

Stakeholders, personas & current-state maps

We used our interview data and proposed interventions to build three family personas and one provider persona. They helped us separate the needs of the decision maker, the organiser, the grieving relative, and the person delivering the service.

The traditionalist

Kalpan

Primary decision maker

49 / Male · Bank manager
Bangalore · Wife + 2 kids

“I have to do this right for my father, but everyone is asking for money and I don't know who to trust.”

Goals

Meet religious and social obligations through ritual accuracy, control costs himself, and coordinate individual vendors. He is wary of agencies “looting money.”

Pain points

  • Tipping culture: repeated ₹1,000–₹3,000 demands for “maintenance” or “rituals” at the crematorium.
  • Vendor chaos: paying and coordinating 5–6 separate providers, including flowers, priest, bamboo bed, and hearse.
  • Administrative opacity: death certificates and bank work become difficult without a family tree or will.

Relationship with technology & services

Low tech adoption; relies on calls and physical presence. Prefers family-managed arrangements to an end-to-end agency.

Original persona sheet ↗

Modern coordinator

Rahul

The organiser

31 / Male · Product designer
Bangalore · Wife / No kids

“I just want to get this done with dignity and without the chaos. Just tell me the price and handle it.”

Goals

Prioritise efficiency and dignity over every minute ritual. Pay a premium for a transparent “One-Call” guarantee, without hidden tips or bribes, and speak to one person instead of ten vendors.

Pain points

  • Local support: no deep family network in the city to help.
  • Handover: a disorganized transition to the furnace and unclear ash collection leave him “in the dark.”
  • Paperwork: weeks of delay around the death certificate and hospital release.

Relationship with technology & services

Uses Google Reviews to find 5-star services and prefers WhatsApp updates. His persona favours electric cremation for cleanliness and the documented time comparison of 1.5 hours versus 6 hours.

Original persona sheet ↗

Grieving Sneha

Sneha

Immediate family

25 / Female · Marketing
Delhi · Lives with parents

“I am in shock. I don't understand what is happening, and the noise is making it worse.”

Goals

Have emotional space without managing logistics, receive gentle guidance during a “psychological breakdown,” and let distant relatives participate through digital broadcasts.

Pain points

  • Insensitivity: aggressive relatives who “scream and beat their chest,” and staff who lack empathy.
  • Information black hole: anxiety about what happens next, with no formal updates.
  • Waiting: long, difficult waits for the hearse or relatives to arrive.

Relationship with technology & services

A passive user: she does not book the service, but experiences it. She values “well-maintained gardens,” peaceful spaces, and a professional funeral director who can buffer the chaos.

Original persona sheet ↗

Hardworking Maddy

Maddy

End-to-end service provider

52 / Male · Entrepreneur
Bangalore · Wife + kids

“I view this work as a blessing rather than just a corporate salary. Much like a doctor or tailor, I am remembered for making a difficult situation better for the family.”

Goals & service model

Motivated by losing his father, he acts as a platform mitigator, similar to Swiggy or Zomato, rather than owning assets. He subcontracts vendors and priests, trains their customer interactions, and offers fixed packages.

Pain points

  • Trust: families can see companies as “looting money.”
  • Niche market: his persona describes less than 1% organized services and 99% unorganized provision.
  • Bottlenecks: government-document delays are outside his control.
  • Cultural complexity: serving 3,000+ communities requires priests who can adapt to different ritual requirements.

05 / The five service concepts

A3, pp. 11–12 ↗

From the clusters to five connected service concepts

We developed each cluster into a concept, then connected the interventions across pre-planning, crisis response, funeral coordination, and longer-term remembrance.

01

Digitization of processes

Intuitive Institutional Interfaces

Redesign existing government interfaces and funeral-related policy so hospitals, municipal authorities, and financial and legal institutions can connect with the Funeral Buddy Coordinator.

We proposed automating death registration and estate or will handoffs through that connected workflow, so paperwork would not become a second trauma.

02

Empathetic & accessible design

  • Integrated Crisis Communication: alert emergency services and primary contacts at the point of crisis.
  • Funeral Buddy Coordinator: a guided assistant to reduce cognitive load while arranging a funeral.
  • Samagri Box: a pre-curated ritual kit available through the market, replacing the need to source materials from 5–6 vendors.
03

Pre-planning

Record end-of-life preferences and primary contacts months or years before they are needed, so relatives have fewer decisions to guess at during bereavement.

The choices include Immediate Support Kit presets and Eco-Legacy Preparation, linking an individual’s wishes to the support and disposition they would choose.

04

Sustainability

Eco-Conscious Disposition

We explored Promession as an experimental alternative. The concept description in A3 outlines freeze-drying remains using liquid nitrogen at −196°C, vibrating the brittle body into powder, drying it, and placing it in a shallow grave, with a proposed return to soil over 6–12 months.

Space-Efficient Memorialization

Vertical or multilayered memorial gardens address limited land in dense cities and create space for remembrance.

05

Grief management

Extend support into the months after the funeral through a scheduled Physical Care Package, Legacy as a Memento, and a Smell Library preserving personal scent profiles.

These concepts explore comfort and sensory remembrance over time, linking practical care with ways of remembering someone beyond the immediate rituals.

The dots reproduce our A3 map’s 1–5 feasibility scores: 3 / 5 for a long-term plan, 4 / 5 for the crisis phase, and 5 / 5 for concepts we considered implementable at the time.

Pre-death

07 / Future scope & limitations

A3, p. 15 ↗

What we wanted to explore next

Our final submission recorded four directions that the semester’s time and scope did not allow us to develop.

  1. Cultural localization

    Partner with local cultural experts to expand kits such as the Samagri Box beyond Hindu rites and support more communities and faiths.

  2. Digital footprint management

    Extend digitization beyond legal and administrative paperwork to digital asset management and an individual’s online footprint after death.

  3. AI Choice Architect

    Explore a choice architect within the Funeral Buddy’s pre-planning phase to help navigate preferences and reduce decision fatigue.

  4. Closed-loop reforestation

    Connect disposition and memorialization with urban reforestation, developing a closed ecological loop from death to life.

Limitations / From our final submission

Facilitating traditions & recognizing human work

“We acknowledge that rituals vary by caste, religion, and region. Our system acts as a medium, not a Dictator; it facilitates the family’s chosen tradition rather than imposing a modernized version.”
“No technology can replace the physical labor of death care. Our documentation focuses on the management of these acts, acknowledging that the Last Mile will always require human hands and empathy.”
